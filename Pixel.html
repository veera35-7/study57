Pixels, Cameras & Printing

The hidden science behind every image you see — screen or paper

How does a photo become a photo? How does a printer turn code into colour on paper? Let's go from the tiniest dot on your screen to the ink on a page. Simple, visual, and beginner-friendly.

What Is a Pixel?

The smallest dot that makes up any digital image

Zoom into any photo far enough and you'll see tiny squares. Each square is a pixel — a "picture element." Every image on your screen is just millions of these coloured dots arranged in a grid.

Light enters through a lens, the shutter opens for a tiny moment, and a sensor made of millions of tiny light-detectors captures it. The chip converts light into electrical signals and turns them into a digital image file.

Light Becomes Data

The sensor converts photons into numbers

💡
Each tiny cell on the sensor measures how much light hit it. Bright cells become high numbers, dark cells become low ones. That grid of numbers is your digital photo — pure math, no magic.

Resolution = Detail

More pixels = sharper image. Fewer pixels = blurrier image.

Low Resolution
512 × 512 pixels
High Resolution
4096 × 4096 pixels
A photo's detail is called resolution — how many pixels it holds. Your phone might take 12 million pixels (12 MP). More pixels = you can zoom in without it turning into mush.

DPI means "Dots Per Inch." A screen only needs ~72 DPI to look smooth. Printers need 300 DPI for a crisp photo — because ink dots on paper must be packed much tighter than glowing pixels.

Mono, Gray & Colour

Three ways a pixel can remember what it saw

◼
Monochrome
Only black or white. 1 bit per pixel.
▓
Grayscale
256 shades of gray. 8 bits per pixel.
🌺
Full Colour
Millions of colours. 24 bits per pixel.
A black-and-white scan only needs 1 bit per pixel. A grayscale photo needs 8 bits. A full-colour photo needs 24 bits — that's why colour files are much bigger.

RGB — Light Colours

Screens mix Red, Green, Blue light to make every colour

Red Green Blue
Your screen has tiny red, green, and blue lights in every pixel. When all three are on full, you see white. When all are off, you see black. Every colour on screen is a mix of these three.

Screens add light (RGB). Printers subtract light using ink. They mix Cyan, Magenta, Yellow — and add Black ink (K) for deep shadows. That's why colours look slightly different on paper than on screen.

A printer takes your RGB design, converts it to CMYK, then breaks every tone into tiny dots called halftones. It layers cyan, magenta, yellow, and black ink — and your eye blends them into smooth colour.

Screen vs Paper

Why they look different — and how to work with both

🖥️ Screen (RGB)

  • Emits light — colours glow
  • Uses Red, Green, Blue
  • ~72 DPI is enough
  • Colours look more vivid
  • Backlit, so brightness changes colour

📄 Print (CMYK)

  • Reflects light — colours sit on paper
  • Uses Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Black
  • 300+ DPI needed for clarity
  • Colours look softer & earthy
  • Paper texture affects the look
A screen glows. Paper reflects. That's the whole difference. If a colour looks amazing on screen but dull in print — that's why. Good designers always check with a printed proof.
